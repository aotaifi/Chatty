\title{Reconstructing frustrated many-body signs from amplitudes with fixed-node and Krylov updates}

\author{A. Otaifi}
\affiliation{Arnold Sommerfeld Center for Theoretical Physics, Ludwig-Maximilians-Universit\"at M\"unchen, 80333 M\"unchen, Germany}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
The sign structure of a frustrated many-body wave function is difficult to
sample and difficult to learn, even when its positive amplitude is already
accurate. We study whether the two tasks can be separated in the square-lattice
spin-$1/2$ $J_1$--$J_2$ Heisenberg model. Writing a real wave function as
$\psi(x)=s(x)a(x)$ with $a(x)>0$, we update the sign from a single Krylov
vector, while fixed-node projection supplies information about the amplitude.

For a given amplitude, the sign of $(T-H)\psi$ is determined by a scalar
threshold of the local energy. On a $4\times4$ exact-diagonalization
benchmark at $J_2/J_1=0.5$, this one-step rule reduces the exact wrong-sign
probability mass from $1.27\times10^{-2}$ to $3.41\times10^{-4}$ and the
fixed-amplitude energy error from $1.16\times10^{-2}$ to
$5.51\times10^{-4}$ per site. On $6\times6$, with the positive amplitude
held fixed, the reconstructed signs reproduce the energy of a trained neural
quantum-state sign structure within the sampling uncertainty. With those
signs, fixed-node projection remains stable on $8\times8$ and yields an
energy in the same range as high-accuracy variational benchmarks.

The converse step can also be closed. On $4\times4$, an exact fixed-node
amplitude followed by the Krylov sign update recovers the exact ground-state
signs from a nonfrustrated starting state. For sampled fixed-node walkers,
maximum likelihood has an exact target:
$a_{\rm new}\propto\sqrt{a\phi_{\FN}}$. At $8\times8$, ordinary
Euclidean likelihood gradients from independent walker populations are
strongly inconsistent, whereas a stochastic-reconfiguration direction
improves a held-out population. These results identify one-step sign
reconstruction as the simple part of the loop and the conversion of
fixed-node walkers into a transferable callable amplitude as the remaining
scaling problem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The sign problem is one of the central obstacles to Monte Carlo calculations
of frustrated quantum matter. In a basis where off-diagonal matrix elements
cannot all be made nonpositive, positive and negative contributions cancel,
and the cost of direct sampling can grow exponentially
\cite{HeneliusSandvik2000,TroyerWiese2005}. The square-lattice
$J_1$--$J_2$ Heisenberg antiferromagnet is a standard example.

At $J_2=0$ the Marshall sign rule fixes the ground-state sign structure on
the bipartite square lattice \cite{Marshall1955}. Around
$J_2/J_1=1/2$, frustration invalidates that simple rule and simultaneously
makes the model a demanding benchmark for modern variational methods,
including neural quantum states \cite{Choo2019,ChenHeyl2024}.

A common response is to improve the entire wave function at once. Neural
quantum states can represent both amplitudes and signs
\cite{CarleoTroyer2017}, while fixed-node methods avoid the sign problem by
holding a trial sign structure fixed \cite{vanBemmel1994}. Here we ask a
different question: can the sign and amplitude be improved by different
numerical operations?

The answer suggested by our data is yes. Given a positive amplitude
$a(x)$ and a current sign pattern $s(x)$, the sign of one Krylov vector
$(T-H)as$ can be read off from a scalar local quantity. Conversely, once
the sign is fixed, a lattice fixed-node projector can improve the positive
amplitude without a sign problem. This gives the feedback structure shown
in Fig.~\ref{fig:method}. The method is not an attempt to make the original
Hamiltonian stoquastic. It instead alternates between a sign-free amplitude
problem and an explicit sign-changing update.

$\psi_k=s_k a_k$ is projected with its signs held fixed. The resulting
positive-amplitude information is converted into a new callable amplitude,
and only then is a one-step Krylov update used to change the signs.}
\label{fig:method}
\end{figure}

Our tests are organized to isolate the two parts of this loop. Exact
diagonalization first asks whether one Krylov step reconstructs the signs when
the amplitude is known. A same-amplitude $6\times6$ comparison then asks
whether the reconstructed signs have the same physical effect as the signs of
a trained neural quantum state. An exact $4\times4$ feedback loop tests
whether fixed-node amplitude improvement and sign reconstruction can
self-correct when composed. Finally, $8\times8$ calculations identify the
practical bottleneck: converting fixed-node walkers into a callable amplitude
that generalizes beyond one walker population.

\section{Model and update rules}

We consider
\begin{equation}
H=J_1\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\mathbf S_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf S_j
 +J_2\sum_{\langle\!\langle ij\rangle\!\rangle}\mathbf S_i\!\cdot\!\mathbf S_j ,
\label{eq:H}
\end{equation}
on $L\times L$ periodic square lattices, with $J_1=1$. Calculations are
performed in the $S^z=0$ sector. Since Eq.~\eqref{eq:H} is real, the wave
function can be written
\begin{equation}
\psi(x)=s(x)a(x),\qquad s(x)=\pm1,\quad a(x)>0,
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
for spin configurations $x$.

When the exact ground state $\psi_0=s_0a_0$ is available, we quantify the sign
error by
\begin{equation}
O_s=\left|\sum_x a_0(x)^2 s(x)s_0(x)\right|,
\qquad
w_s=\frac{1-O_s}{2}.
\label{eq:signoverlap}
\end{equation}
After fixing the irrelevant global sign, $w_s$ is simply the exact
ground-state probability carried by configurations with the wrong sign.
For larger systems, where exact signs are unavailable, we instead compare
energies at fixed amplitude or fixed-node projected energies.

\subsection{One-step Krylov sign reconstruction}

For a signed guide $\psi=sa$, define its local energy
\begin{equation}
r_s(x)=\frac{(H\psi)(x)}{\psi(x)}.
\label{eq:rs}
\end{equation}
A single shifted Krylov vector satisfies
\begin{equation}
[(T-H)\psi](x)=[T-r_s(x)]\psi(x).
\end{equation}
Therefore its sign is obtained without evaluating a new many-body amplitude:
\begin{equation}
s_{\rm K}(x)=s(x)\,\operatorname{sgn}[T-r_s(x)].
\label{eq:k1}
\end{equation}
The amplitude factor $|T-r_s|a$ is discarded. We retain only the sign and
restore or re-solve the amplitude before another sign update. This distinction
is important: repeated Krylov multiplication is not the algorithm studied
here.

The shift $T$ acts as a one-dimensional decision threshold. In exact
small-system tests we choose it by minimizing the fixed-amplitude variational
energy after the sign update; this uses the Hamiltonian but no target sign
labels. On sampled larger systems we determine $T$ from the distribution of
$r_s$ itself with a robust two-cluster split. Exact or neural signs are used
only afterward as diagnostics.

\subsection{Fixed-node amplitude update}

For a guide $\psi_G(x)=s(x)a(x)$, define the sign-violating neighbors
\begin{equation}
\mathcal V_x=
\left\{y\neq x:
H_{xy}\frac{\psi_G(y)}{\psi_G(x)}>0
\right\}.
\end{equation}
The lattice fixed-node Hamiltonian removes every sign-violating hopping
term and transfers its contribution to the diagonal:
\begin{align}
(H_{\FN})_{xy}&=
\begin{cases}
H_{xy}, & y\notin\mathcal V_x,\\
0, & y\in\mathcal V_x,
\end{cases}
\qquad x\neq y,\\
(H_{\FN})_{xx}&=
H_{xx}+\sum_{y\in\mathcal V_x}
H_{xy}\frac{\psi_G(y)}{\psi_G(x)} .
\label{eq:fn}
\end{align}
After importance sampling with the guide, $H_{\FN}$ is sign free
\cite{vanBemmel1994}. Its ground state has the imposed sign pattern $s(x)$
and a positive modulus that we denote by $\phi_{\FN}(x)$.

On an exact small system one may simply feed that modulus back into
Eq.~\eqref{eq:k1}. In a walker calculation, however, the projector does not
return a table of $\phi_{\FN}(x)$. With guide amplitude $a(x)$, the
stationary mixed distribution is
\begin{equation}
f(x)\propto a(x)\phi_{\FN}(x).
\label{eq:mixed}
\end{equation}
If a positive model is trained by maximum likelihood with Born probability
$p_\theta(x)\propto a_\theta(x)^2$, the ideal maximum-likelihood target is
therefore
\begin{equation}
a_{\rm new}(x)\propto
\sqrt{a(x)\phi_{\FN}(x)} .
\label{eq:halfstep}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:halfstep} is the precise amplitude handoff that we later
benchmark. It is a geometric-mean, or half, step toward the current
fixed-node amplitude; because $H_{\FN}$ itself depends on the guide ratios,
the target changes after every accepted update.

\subsection{Numerical protocol}

The exact $4\times4$ calculations use the full $S^z=0$ Hilbert space of
dimension $12\,870$. For $6\times6$ and $8\times8$ we use a previously
optimized translationally invariant complex vision-transformer (ViT) guide at
$J_2/J_1=0.5$, with eight transformer layers, embedding dimension 60 and ten
attention heads. In the controlled sign benchmarks we keep its modulus
$a(x)=|\psi_{\rm ViT}(x)|$ fixed and replace only its sign; the neural state
is not retrained during that comparison.

The $8\times8$ fixed-node calculations use population $M=128$ and projection
length $\beta=1.2$. Whenever only two independent fixed-node populations are
available, we show both population means and use half their difference as a
reproducibility scale. We do not interpret that two-population spread as a
precision statistical error bar. Sampling and estimator details for each
plotted quantity are retained with the source data in the accompanying
reproducibility files.

\section{One Krylov step reconstructs the important signs}
\label{sec:k1}

We first isolate the sign problem by supplying a high-quality positive
amplitude and changing only the signs. Figure~\ref{fig:signbench}(a,b) uses
the exact $4\times4$ ground-state modulus. For $J_2/J_1\leq0.6$ the parent
sign is the Marshall pattern; on the strongly stripe-dominated side we use
the corresponding stripe reference. Thus the sweep tests reconstruction
within the physically appropriate sign basin rather than asking a single
Marshall reference to describe every coupling.

At the maximally frustrated point $J_2/J_1=0.5$, the Marshall sign overlap is
$O_s=0.974538$, corresponding to
$w_s=1.27\times10^{-2}$. A single update raises the overlap to
$0.999319$ and leaves only $w_s=3.41\times10^{-4}$. The same change reduces
the fixed-amplitude energy error from $1.16\times10^{-2}$ to
$5.51\times10^{-4}$ per site. Thus the improvement is not merely a better
classification score: at fixed exact amplitude it removes almost all of the
energy penalty associated with the wrong signs.

The $6\times6$ panel provides a less oracle-dependent test. We take the
positive amplitude from a trained ViT neural quantum state and evaluate three
signed states with that \emph{same} amplitude. Their energies per site are
\begin{align}
e_{\rm Marshall}&=-0.4981534,\\
e_{\rm Krylov}&=-0.5033337,\\
e_{\rm ViT}&=-0.5034504.
\end{align}
The paired difference between the Krylov and ViT signs is
$e_{\rm Krylov}-e_{\rm ViT}=1.17(287)\times10^{-4}$, whereas the Krylov
signs improve over Marshall by $4.79\times10^{-3}$ per site. Because the
amplitude is identical in all three evaluations, this comparison isolates the
physical quality of the sign structure.

\subsection{Why does one step work so well?}

The local quantity in Eq.~\eqref{eq:rs} can be decomposed in the Marshall
gauge as
\begin{equation}
r_M(x)=D(x)+F_{J_1}(x)+F_{J_2}(x),
\label{eq:decompfield}
\end{equation}
where $D$ is diagonal, $F_{J_1}$ is the unfrustrated nearest-neighbor hopping
contribution, and $F_{J_2}$ is the frustrating next-nearest-neighbor
contribution. The useful sign information is carried predominantly by the
competition of the two off-diagonal terms, weighted by neighboring
amplitudes, rather than by a classical diagonal motif.

Figure~\ref{fig:signbench}(d) makes this separation visible on $8\times8$.
Although the two distributions overlap, the sign-defect population is
strongly biased toward one side of the scalar threshold. On the independent
$6\times6$ mechanism audit, the physically weighted wrong-sign mass changes
from $0.01635$ to $8.08\times10^{-4}$ on the training sample and from
$0.01811$ to $8.02\times10^{-4}$ on validation. The first Krylov step
therefore removes about $95\%$ of the relevant sign-defect weight.

This success does not imply that repeated bare Krylov steps form a convergent
sign solver. The first Krylov vector also multiplies the amplitude by
$|T-r_s(x)|$. If that distorted amplitude is retained, a second unsupervised
threshold acts on a residual defect population that is already extremely
small and is dominated by amplitude and local-energy outliers. Direct
two-step tests on $6\times6$ worsen the fixed-amplitude energy. This is why
our feedback loop returns to a fixed-node amplitude solve before asking for
another sign update; quantitative two-step data are summarized in
Appendix~\ref{app:k2}.

\section{Exact closure of the fixed-node--Krylov loop}
\label{sec:exactloop}

The previous section assumed that a useful positive amplitude was available.
We next ask whether the two operations in Fig.~\ref{fig:method} can generate
each other's input. This can be tested without sampling noise on $4\times4$.
We target $J_2/J_1=0.5$ but initialize the guide with the exact
\emph{nonfrustrated} ($J_2=0$) ground-state modulus and Marshall signs.
At each iteration we solve the lattice fixed-node problem exactly for the
current guide, use its positive modulus as the next amplitude, and then
recompute the one-step sign rule of Eq.~\eqref{eq:k1}. The target frustrated
ground state is used only to measure the errors plotted below.

The result is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:closedloop}. The initial amplitude
fidelity to the target state is only $0.7132$, while the initial sign overlap
is the Marshall value $0.974538$. The fixed-node amplitude improves rapidly;
sign changes then occur in discrete bursts when the updated amplitude moves
the local-energy threshold across a new set of configurations. After two
iterations the sign overlap is already $0.995945$, after twenty iterations it
is $0.9999982$, and by iteration 100 the exact target sign pattern is reached.
At that point the amplitude fidelity is $0.9999946$ and the guide energy lies
only $2.60\times10^{-5}$ above the exact ground-state energy.

The staircase structure in panel (a) is useful conceptually. The sign update
does not need to improve at every iteration. Fixed-node projection can first
reshape the positive amplitude while the sign pattern remains unchanged;
once enough amplitude information has accumulated, Eq.~\eqref{eq:k1}
crosses another sign boundary. The exact loop therefore demonstrates genuine
feedback between the two sectors rather than repeated application of the same
sign rule to a frozen amplitude.

\section{Scaling the sign update to $8\times8$}
\label{sec:scale}

The exact loop is a proof of principle, not a scalable implementation,
because a generic amplitude cannot be tabulated on large lattices. Before
addressing that handoff, we first test whether the sign update itself remains
useful when the system size is increased.

On $8\times8$ we start from the same trained ViT amplitude used to construct
the one-step sign guide and perform lattice fixed-node projection with
population $M=128$. Two independent Krylov-sign fixed-node runs give total
energies $-31.88702$ and $-31.88383$, while matched Marshall-sign controls
give $-31.70383$ and $-31.65113$. The mean difference is
\begin{equation}
E_{\rm Krylov\text{-}FN}-E_{\rm Marshall\text{-}FN}
=-0.20795 ,
\end{equation}
with a two-replica spread proxy of $0.02640$. We use this spread only as a
reproducibility diagnostic; with two populations it is not a precision
asymptotic uncertainty estimate.

Figure~\ref{fig:8x8bench} places this result next to published calculations
on the identical $8\times8$ periodic geometry. The Krylov-sign fixed-node
mean is
\begin{equation}
E/N=-0.498210 ,
\end{equation}
compared with $-0.494961$ for the Marshall-sign fixed-node control. The
Krylov-guided value lies in the same narrow energy range as the high-bond
dimension DMRG/FAMPS and RBM+pair-product benchmarks compiled in
Ref.~\cite{QianQin2023}. We do not interpret the small ordering among these
numbers as a precision ranking: our fixed-node result has finite-population
systematics and only two independent populations. The useful conclusion is
more limited and more robust: the one-step reconstructed sign structure
survives to $8\times8$ and removes most of the large energy penalty of the
Marshall signs.

At this point the bottleneck changes. Fixed-node projection can be sampled at
$8\times8$, and the one-step sign rule can be evaluated from a callable
guide. What is missing is a scalable map from a cloud of fixed-node walkers
back to a callable positive amplitude. A low-dimensional histogram correction
works as a diagnostic on smaller lattices, but nested evaluation of such
corrections becomes prohibitively expensive at $8\times8$. We therefore turn
to direct learning of the amplitude update in Eq.~\eqref{eq:halfstep}.

\section{Learning the fixed-node amplitude from walkers}
\label{sec:learning}

Equation~\eqref{eq:halfstep} provides an exact target for the learning
problem. Before using a neural network, we can apply that geometric-mean
update exactly on $4\times4$. Figure~\ref{fig:amplearn}(a) shows two
initial conditions. Starting from the exact target modulus but Marshall signs,
the first ideal maximum-likelihood half step followed by the Krylov sign
update changes the energy from $-8.27291$ to $-8.44579$, close to the exact
$E_0=-8.45792$. Starting instead from a uniform modulus is deliberately much
harder: the first update is nonmonotone, but subsequent fixed-node/half-step
iterations recover and approach the correct low-energy basin. Thus the
geometric-mean target is locally contracting toward each current fixed-node
amplitude, but it does not define a global energy-descent theorem.

The finite-sample problem is more subtle. We fine-tune the existing ViT
amplitude rather than replacing it with a newly initialized network. Let
$g_1$ and $g_2$ denote maximum-likelihood gradients estimated from two
independent $M=128$ fixed-node populations. At the initial $8\times8$ guide,
their Euclidean cosine is
\begin{equation}
\frac{g_1\!\cdot g_2}{\|g_1\|\|g_2\|}=-0.853 .
\end{equation}
A naive Euclidean update therefore points in a direction that does not
generalize even at infinitesimal step size.

We instead use stochastic reconfiguration (SR) \cite{Sorella2001}.
With empirical Fisher matrix $S$ and diagonal shift $\lambda$, its update is
\begin{equation}
\delta\theta=(S+\lambda I)^{-1}g_1 .
\label{eq:sr}
\end{equation}
Using $\lambda=1$ and a matrix-free solve, the same two populations give the
held-out directional derivative
\begin{equation}
g_2^{\mathsf T}\delta\theta=+3.85 ,
\end{equation}
despite the negative Euclidean cosine. The finite line search in
Fig.~\ref{fig:amplearn}(b) confirms that this is not merely an infinitesimal
effect: at $\eta=0.01$ the held-out log-likelihood gain is $+0.0156$, while
the importance-sampling effective sample size remains $3236$ out of $4096$.

This is the scalable amplitude result established here. It shows that
fixed-node walker information can be converted into a neural-amplitude
direction that transfers between independent populations. It does
\emph{not} yet establish convergence of a repeatedly learned $8\times8$
fixed-node--Krylov loop. In particular, likelihood improvement is a surrogate
for the amplitude handoff, whereas the projected fixed-node energy remains
the physical acceptance criterion for future iterations.

\section{Discussion}

The numerical picture that emerges is simpler than trying to learn an entire
frustrated wave function in one operation. Once a useful positive amplitude
is available, the dominant correction to the sign structure is already
encoded in the amplitude-weighted local action of the Hamiltonian. A single
shifted Krylov vector converts that information into a scalar threshold
problem. Exact diagonalization shows that this removes almost all of the
sign-defect weight at $J_2/J_1=0.5$, and the same-amplitude $6\times6$ test
shows that the reconstructed signs have essentially the same energetic effect
as the signs of the trained neural state.

Fixed-node projection plays a complementary role. It does not infer a new sign
pattern: by construction it keeps the current signs fixed and solves a
sign-free positive-amplitude problem. The sign change occurs only when the
resulting amplitude is inserted back into Eq.~\eqref{eq:k1}. The exact
$4\times4$ calculation demonstrates that this composition can cross many
distinct sign patterns and eventually recover the exact frustrated ground
state from a nonfrustrated starting point.

The scaling tests separate two questions that are often conflated. The
Krylov sign rule itself remains useful through $8\times8$, where its
fixed-node energy is competitive with established same-geometry variational
benchmarks. The difficult step is instead statistical: a fixed-node walker
population represents the mixed distribution of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mixed}, not a
callable wave-function modulus. Direct Euclidean maximum-likelihood gradients
are population dependent at $8\times8$. A natural-gradient/SR metric repairs
that first transfer test, but a robust repeated large-system feedback loop
still requires a physical acceptance test and controlled population
statistics.

Three boundaries of the present result are therefore important. First, the
one-step rule is not a generic solution of the sign problem: it requires a
useful starting amplitude and a parent sign basin. Second, the excellent first
step should not be extrapolated to an undamped Krylov sequence; the direct
second step is demonstrably worse. Third, the exact small-system closure does
not prove polynomial scaling of the learned amplitude handoff. What it does
establish is a concrete decomposition of the problem in which sign
reconstruction is inexpensive and quantitatively accurate, while the
remaining hard object---a transferable positive amplitude learned from
projector data---can be isolated and tested independently.

\section{Conclusion}

For the frustrated square-lattice $J_1$--$J_2$ model, accurate amplitudes
contain enough local Hamiltonian information to reconstruct most of the
non-Marshall sign structure with one projected Krylov step. Exact
diagonalization, same-amplitude neural-state tests, and $8\times8$
fixed-node calculations all support this statement. Fixed-node projection
can in turn improve the amplitude without a sign problem, and on $4\times4$
the two updates form a self-correcting loop that reaches the exact signs.

For large systems the central unresolved issue is no longer how to propose a
sign change, but how to turn sampled fixed-node amplitude information into a
stable callable guide. The mixed-distribution identity
$a_{\rm new}\propto\sqrt{a\phi_{\FN}}$ gives the correct learning target,
and stochastic reconfiguration produces a direction that transfers between
independent $8\times8$ walker populations. Closing that learned loop
repeatedly, with fixed-node energy rather than likelihood as the acceptance
criterion, is the remaining step.

\section{Why a direct second Krylov step is not used}
\label{app:k2}

A natural alternative to the feedback construction would be to retain the
full first Krylov vector and apply a second Krylov step. We tested this
directly on $6\times6$. The first operation changes not only the signs but
also the modulus,
\begin{equation}
a_1(x)=|T_1-r_0(x)|a_0(x),
\end{equation}
whose multiplier has a coefficient of variation of approximately $0.18$ on
the physical diagnostic sample.

Across three unsupervised second-step thresholds, the physical sign-change
mass is only $0.8$--$2.0\%$, but the fixed-amplitude energy rises by
$0.65$--$0.96$ in total energy relative to the one-step signs. The residual
correctable sign defects are much rarer than the population selected by an
ordinary second threshold. Restoring the original high-quality amplitude
removes most of the directly observed bad second-step flips. We therefore
interpret the failure as amplitude distortion and rare-tail sensitivity, not
as evidence that the dominant first-step sign correction was spurious.

\section{Statistical conventions}

Exact-diagonalization quantities are deterministic up to numerical
eigensolver precision. For the $6\times6$ fixed-amplitude comparison we use
independent chains and report a correlation-robust between-chain standard
error. For $8\times8$ fixed-node calculations the manuscript reports two
independent populations. The quantity shown as an error bar is the
between-population spread divided by two, equal to the usual standard-error
formula for two samples. Because two populations are insufficient to
establish asymptotic normal statistics, we refer to this quantity throughout
as a \emph{two-replica spread proxy}, not as a precision statistical
uncertainty.
